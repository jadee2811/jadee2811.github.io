How Southeast Asian retailers are rethinking growth at scale

Growth creates demand. Execution determines how much of that demand becomes value, and AI helps most when it removes a constraint already limiting growth.

By Jade · August 2026

Over the past year, while looking at retail businesses across Vietnam and Southeast Asia, one pattern has become increasingly clear.

Growth is getting harder to capture

Most retailers are no longer struggling to build digital channels. Beauty, health & beauty, electronics and convenience retailers already operate across websites, marketplaces, social commerce, CRM, loyalty programs and physical stores. Customers are interacting with brands across more touchpoints than ever.

Yet growth is becoming harder to capture. More customers can mean higher acquisition costs. More orders can mean higher fulfillment and service costs. More promotions can increase volume while putting pressure on margins. And more channels can create more customer data without necessarily giving the business a clearer understanding of what each customer actually needs.

This brings me back to a question I have written about in previous editions: revenue growth is not necessarily healthy growth. The question is no longer simply how to generate more demand. It is: how do we create more value from the demand we already have, without allowing the cost and complexity of growth to increase at the same rate?

Looking at several retailers across Southeast Asia, I see the answer starting with a simple idea: growth creates demand. Execution determines how much of that demand becomes value.

When growth works, the next challenge is scale

Take Hasaki. Its growth model combines physical distribution, a broad beauty portfolio, competitive pricing, promotions and multiple digital channels. With 328 stores and more than 500 brands, it has built significant reach in a market where consumers are increasingly comfortable comparing products and prices across platforms.

But once customers have more choices, more traffic does not automatically create more conversion. The challenge shifts from acquiring the customer to helping the customer decide. Which product is right for me? Which one fits my budget? What should I combine it with? Is there a better option? This is a familiar transition in retail: when acquisition becomes more expensive, creating more value from each customer interaction becomes a growth lever in itself.

Guardian Malaysia illustrates another version of the same idea. Malaysia is already a highly digital retail market, yet Guardian continues to combine a large physical footprint with pharmacist expertise and in-store consultation. With 554 stores across Malaysia, including around 100 with pharmacists, Guardian uses pharmacy credibility, product education and consultation as part of its health and beauty proposition. Asia Circles describes this model as connecting digital discovery with physical experience and expert advice.

What I think other retailers can learn from this is straightforward. When customers have hundreds of choices, helping them make the right decision can become a competitive advantage in itself. But this creates a natural limit. If consultation creates value, then the capacity of the expert becomes a finite resource. A pharmacist or beauty advisor can only serve a certain number of customers each day. So the question is no longer whether consultation works. It becomes “How do you scale a capability that is already creating value?”

That is where AI becomes interesting. Not to replace the expert, but to extend their capacity by helping with initial discovery, product matching, recommendations and follow-up, while human expertise remains focused where judgment and trust matter most.

The lesson is bigger than Guardian: when a capability becomes a growth lever, its capacity becomes part of the growth equation.

The same pattern appears in electronics

At Thế Giới Di Động, the challenge looks different but the underlying problem is similar. The business already has product breadth, customer traffic and sales expertise. The challenge is making that knowledge useful across a very large number of customer decisions.

A case published by Easy AI reports more than 120,000 customers supported per month, more than 25,000 messages per day, and information across more than 100,000 products. At that scale, product information is not the constraint. The constraint is turning product information into a useful decision for each customer. A customer may not want every specification of a phone. They may want to know which phone makes sense for their budget, usage and priorities.

That makes the problem less about information retrieval and more about decision support. AI can help turn product knowledge into scalable sales capacity: understanding customer intent, connecting it with product context, comparing alternatives and supporting the next step toward purchase. The important point is not that AI is answering questions. It is that product knowledge is being brought into the revenue workflow.

The same principle appears from the service side at Điện máy XANH (another brand of MWG). In home appliances, customer questions can cover products, pricing, promotions, availability, delivery, installation, warranty and stores. A case published by Easy AI reports more than 15,000 messages per day, with CSAT above 90%.

At this volume, adding people every time interaction volume increases is difficult to sustain. The opportunity is to separate repetitive, high-volume interactions from situations that require human judgment.

AI handles the repetition. People handle the relationship. The goal is not maximum automation. The goal is to maximize the value created by every human hour.

Regional growth creates another constraint

The same issue becomes more complex when retailers expand across Southeast Asia. Kinohimitsu, with more than 5,000 points of sale across the region, shows how distribution can be scaled across different markets. Safi provides another example of localized growth, with halal positioning, affordability and cultural relevance playing an important role in Malaysia.

But Southeast Asia is not one market. A successful playbook in Vietnam cannot simply be copied into Malaysia, Indonesia or Thailand. Customer behavior, language, culture, pricing and preferred channels all change. So regional expansion creates another question: “How do you scale without losing local relevance?”

This requires more than centralized strategy. It requires local intelligence and local execution. AI can support that layer by helping businesses analyze customer signals, adapt communication, personalize recommendations and support local teams without requiring every market to rebuild its intelligence from scratch.

Central strategy. Local intelligence. Local execution.

What these cases tell me about AI revenue growth

These businesses have very different growth engines. Hasaki has distribution and price. Guardian has expertise and consultation. Thế Giới Di Động has product breadth. Điện máy XANH has customer service scale. Kinohimitsu and Safi have regional distribution and localization. Yet the underlying problem is remarkably similar. As the business grows, the capability required to execute the growth strategy also grows.

More traffic puts pressure on conversion. More products create decision complexity. More customers create demand for consultation and service. More markets create localization challenges. This is why I would not start an AI discussion with “Where can we use AI?”. I would start with “What is driving our growth today, and what is limiting our ability to scale it?”

If conversion is the issue, look at intent and recommendation. If consultation is the growth lever, look at expert capacity. If product complexity is slowing sales, make product knowledge easier to use. If service volume is growing faster than headcount, automate the repetitive layer. If customer data is fragmented, turn insight into action. If regional expansion is the strategy, scale local relevance.

AI becomes valuable when it removes a constraint that is already limiting growth.

From AI capability to AI revenue execution

This is the next step from my previous edition on AI Access → AI Capability. Having access to AI is not enough, building AI capability is not enough either. The capability has to become part of how the business actually sells, serves and retains customers.

A customer conversation should not end with an answer. It should create context. That context should inform the next recommendation. The recommendation should support a commercial action. The interaction should generate insight. And that insight should improve the next interaction. When these pieces connect, AI stops being a tool sitting alongside the business. It becomes part of the revenue execution layer.

That is also why I believe AI should be measured differently. Not simply by conversations handled or automation rate, but by whether it improves conversion, Average Order Value, repeat purchase, Customer Lifetime Value, cost-to-serve, revenue per employee and margin. These are the numbers that tell us whether AI is actually improving the economics of growth.

Key takeaway

The first stage of AI adoption was about access. The next stage is about capability but the real business value comes when that capability changes execution.

For retail, that means identifying where customer intent is being lost, where human capacity is becoming the constraint, where customer insight is not reaching action, and where the cost of scaling is rising faster than revenue. Then, and only then, deciding where AI belongs. Because the competitive advantage will not come from having access to the same AI models as everyone else. It will come from knowing where to apply them, how to integrate them into the way the business operates, and how to turn better execution into measurable growth.

AI does not create revenue by itself. Better execution does. AI makes that execution scalable.
