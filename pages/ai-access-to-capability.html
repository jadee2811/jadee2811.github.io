From AI access to AI capability: what retailers need to build for revenue growth

Having AI is not the same as having AI capability. The real question is where revenue leaks from the customer journey, and what capability fixes it.

Over the past year, while working with electronics and home appliance retailers, one pattern has consistently emerged. Businesses are investing heavily in attracting more customers, and revenue grows. Yet profitability often does not improve at the same pace.

Revenue leakage

Businesses launch new campaigns, expand product categories, open new stores, add salespeople, and invest in digital channels. Revenue grows. Yet when leadership teams review performance, they often come back to a familiar question: why isn't profitability improving at the same pace?

As I wrote in the previous edition, the answer is often not a lack of demand. It is the operational friction that accumulates between customer intent and revenue.

A salesperson spends five to ten minutes searching for product specifications before answering a customer, then spends more time wording the response. A manager can spend most of a working day preparing a report because the numbers have to be collected and checked across different systems. A customer visits a website looking for a product. If the information is not available quickly enough, or no one responds when they need help, they may leave after one or two minutes and continue their search elsewhere. Marketing generates a lead and passes it to sales. But when the salesperson picks up the phone, they may know the customer's name and contact details without knowing what the customer actually looked at, what they asked, or how strong their purchase intent was.

None of these problems looks dramatic on its own. Together, they create revenue leakage. And this is where I think the conversation about AI needs to move forward. The question is no longer whether a retailer can access AI. Most can. The harder question is: does the organization have the capability to turn AI into better execution, and better execution into measurable revenue?

Having AI is not the same as having AI capability

There is a fundamental difference between an employee using AI and an organization having AI capability. A salesperson can open an AI assistant, search for a product specification, and ask it to rewrite a response to a customer. That is useful but the underlying process has not changed. The salesperson still has to find the information. The customer is still waiting. The conversation may still sit outside the CRM. The next salesperson who handles that customer may still have no context. And management may still have no way of knowing whether the interaction eventually converted.

AI has helped a person complete a task. It has not yet changed the system.

Now imagine a different workflow. A customer arrives on a retailer's website and starts asking about refrigerators. An AI Shopping Assistant can answer product questions immediately, compare specifications, understand what the customer is looking for, and recognize when the conversation is moving from browsing toward purchase intent.

If the customer is ready to speak with a salesperson, the conversation does not start again from zero. The salesperson receives the context. They know what the customer explored, what they asked, which products they considered, and what appears to be driving the purchase decision. The salesperson can then focus on what requires human judgment: building confidence, handling objections, recommending the right option, and closing the sale.

The AI is no longer sitting beside the workflow. It has become part of the workflow.

That distinction matters because individual AI usage does not necessarily create organizational capability. A retailer may have thousands of employees using AI and still operate the same fragmented processes it had before.

The real problem is not access. It is the customer journey

Retailers have spent years building systems around different parts of the customer journey. Marketing has its platforms. Sales has its CRM. E-commerce has its website. Customer service has its tools. Operations has its reporting systems. The technology exists; the problem is that the customer does not experience these systems separately.

The customer experiences one journey. They see an advertisement. They visit a website. They search for a product. They ask a question. They compare options. They leave. They come back. They talk to a salesperson. They receive a quotation. They decide whether to buy.

Inside the organization, however, those moments can be fragmented across teams and systems. A lead may successfully move from marketing to CRM without the customer's intent moving with it. Sales may receive the contact details without knowing what the customer explored. Customer service may have the conversation history without that context becoming useful to the sales team. This is why simply connecting systems is not enough. The real objective is to connect the intelligence around the customer.

And behind that intelligence sits a less visible challenge: data. Customer interactions, product information, transaction history, marketing activity, and sales conversations often live across different systems. When that information remains siloed, AI may be able to answer questions, but it cannot reliably understand the customer journey or recommend what should happen next.

AI capability therefore starts long before the AI model. It starts with making the right business context accessible at the right moment. That means customer data, product knowledge, CRM, commerce, marketing, and service workflows need to work together well enough for AI to understand what happened, what is happening, and what should happen next. Without that foundation, even a powerful AI model is working with an incomplete picture.

The revenue value of speed

This matters even more in retail because customer intent is often short-lived. A customer researching a refrigerator at 10:30 PM may be ready to buy tonight. If they cannot find the information they need, they do not necessarily wait until the next morning. They open another website. The same thing happens when a customer submits an inquiry and waits too long for a response.

From an operational perspective, this looks like a response-time problem. From a commercial perspective, it is a revenue leakage problem. That distinction changes how the business should think about AI. If a retailer only measures response time, it may try to make customer service faster. If it understands the relationship between response time, engagement, lead capture, and conversion, it can ask a much more valuable question: how much revenue are we losing because we cannot respond to customer intent quickly enough?

The same logic applies to sales productivity. Saving a salesperson five minutes on product research may sound like a small efficiency gain. However, multiply that across dozens of customer interactions a day and hundreds of salespeople, and the accumulated productivity becomes significant. More importantly, the time saved can be redirected toward activities that actually create revenue.

AI should not be evaluated only by how much work it removes. It should also be evaluated by what higher-value work it gives people more time to do.

Scale changes the economics

The economics change again as the business grows. For a small retailer, a salesperson manually following up with a customer may be manageable. For a business handling thousands of leads every month, the same process can become a significant source of lost revenue. The same is true for customer support. One unanswered question is one missed opportunity. Multiply that across hundreds of thousands of interactions, and the economics become very different.

We have seen different versions of this model in practice. At Rạng Đông, the D2C operating model supports both dealer and consumer segments across multiple channels. When AI converts an interaction into a lead, sales can receive that lead immediately for follow-up. For smaller consumer transactions, customers can also complete purchases directly through the e-commerce system. The important part is not simply that AI answers questions. It is that the interaction can move directly from customer intent to commercial action.

At the scale of Thế Giới Di Động and Điện Máy XANH, AI has been applied across shopping assistance and customer support to handle very large volumes of customer interactions. In certain workflows, automation has reached up to 98%, helping reduce the workload created by repetitive customer interactions and enabling thousands of leads to be converted automatically each month. But I would not interpret that number as a target for every retailer. A 98% automation rate only makes sense when the economics, transaction volume, customer behavior, product complexity, and operating model support it.

For many small and mid-sized businesses, the better model may be very different. AI can identify intent, answer initial questions, retrieve information, qualify the opportunity, and prepare the context. A human then steps in when the conversation requires judgment or persuasion.

AI handles the repetition. People handle the relationship. The point is not to maximize automation. The point is to maximize the value created by every human hour.

Capability is built around the revenue problem

This brings me back to the revenue metrics I discussed in the previous editions. Once a retailer understands where growth comes from, the next question is whether the organization has the capability to influence those drivers consistently. If conversion is the problem, can the business respond to customer intent quickly enough? If leads are being lost, can every high-intent customer receive timely follow-up? If sales productivity is low, can employees spend less time searching for information and more time selling? If engagement is weak, can the organization respond to customer behavior before the opportunity disappears? If operating costs are increasing faster than revenue, can repetitive work be automated without damaging the customer experience?

These are not really AI questions. They are business questions. AI simply gives organizations a new way to solve them at a scale that was previously difficult to achieve. This is why I increasingly think about AI capability not as a technology stack, but as an organizational capability. It starts with access to AI, then comes the ability to use it consistently in everyday work. Then the harder step: embedding it into workflows, connecting it to customer and business data, giving people the right context to make decisions, and measuring whether those decisions actually change the outcome. Only at that point does AI become something the organization can scale.

The difference between using AI and building capability

This is where many AI initiatives quietly lose momentum. A company launches a chatbot. Employees receive access to an AI assistant. A dashboard is built. A few teams start experimenting. Everyone can point to something called “AI”, but six months later, the underlying workflow has not changed. The same customer information still sits in different systems. The same reports are still prepared manually. The same leads still fall through the cracks. The same salesperson still spends ten minutes looking for product information.

The organization has adopted AI, however, it has not built the capability to use AI systematically. The retailers that move ahead will approach the problem differently. They will start with the revenue leakage. They will identify where customer intent is being lost, where employees are spending time on low-value work, where execution differs between teams or stores, and where information fails to move with the customer.

Then they will redesign the workflow. AI comes after that, not before. Because AI cannot compensate for a broken process. It can, however, make a well-designed process faster, more consistent, more intelligent, and eventually more scalable.

Measure the capability by what changes

Ultimately, I do not think the success of AI in retail should be measured by how many people have access to an AI tool. It should be measured by what changes in the business. Are more leads being captured before customers leave? Are customers engaging for longer because they can get useful answers immediately? Are more conversations turning into qualified opportunities? Are salespeople spending less time searching and more time selling? Are repetitive customer interactions being handled without adding headcount? Are managers spending less time assembling reports and more time making decisions? And most importantly: does the improvement show up in the economics of the business?

Lead, engagement, conversion, cost reduction, automation. These are not just AI metrics. They are signals of whether the organization is becoming better at turning customer intent into revenue. That is the real transition I believe retailers are facing now. Not from traditional business to AI business. But from AI access to AI capability.

Key takeaway

The first wave of AI adoption was about giving people access to better tools. The next wave will be about building organizations that know how to use those tools systematically.

For retail, that means connecting the customer journey, embedding intelligence into workflows, deciding where automation creates value and where humans should remain involved, and measuring every improvement against a real business outcome. Because ultimately, the competitive advantage will not come from having access to the same AI models as everyone else. It will come from knowing where to apply them, how to integrate them into the way the business operates, and how to turn the resulting productivity into measurable growth.

In my view, that is what AI capability really means. Not more AI. Better execution, powered by AI.

The question I would ask any retail leadership team today is not: “Where can we use AI?”. It is: “Where is revenue leaking from our customer journey, and what capability do we need to build to fix it?” That is where the next phase of AI adoption begins.
